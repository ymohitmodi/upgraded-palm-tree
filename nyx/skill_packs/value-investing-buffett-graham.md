# Value Investing Doctrine — Buffett Letters & Graham's Framework

Distilled operating doctrine from Warren Buffett's shareholder letters (1957–2023)
and Benjamin Graham's framework, written for an analyst that must value real
businesses from filings. Concepts, not quotations; every idea below should change
how a company is read, valued, or rejected.

## Mr. Market and the ownership frame

Graham's central device: the market is a manic business partner who quotes you a
price every day. The quote is there to SERVE you, never to instruct you. Two
consequences the analyst must operationalize. (1) A stock is a fractional claim
on a business's future distributable cash — analyze the business as if buying the
whole company at market cap, judging it by what it can pay out over decades, not
by what the next quote will be. (2) Volatility is the source of opportunity, not
the definition of risk: price falling below conservatively appraised value is a
gift; the true risk is PERMANENT capital loss, which comes from overpaying,
deteriorating economics, leverage, or being forced to sell at the bottom. Beta,
tracking error, and quote volatility are irrelevant to a business owner who
controls their own holding period. Institutional behavior (career risk, closet
indexing, quarterly horizons) systematically creates mispricings a patient
individual can harvest — the structural edge is temperament plus time arbitrage,
not information.

## Margin of safety — the engineering discipline

Graham's three most important words. Demand a large discount between price and a
CONSERVATIVE appraisal of intrinsic value, sized to absorb bad luck, estimation
error, and your own bias. Mechanics that make it real rather than rhetorical:
appraise value with sober inputs (normalized earnings, maintenance capex fully
charged, no heroic growth), then require the discount on top — never "find" the
margin by inflating the appraisal. The margin substitutes for certainty: the
wider the range of possible values, the larger the required discount, and if the
business can't be valued within a reasonable range it belongs in the too-hard
pile, not in the portfolio at a bigger discount. Margin of safety also determines
position sizing: the cheapness and certainty justify concentration; diversification
is protection against ignorance and is fine for those who know they don't know.

## Owner earnings — the number that matters

Reported EPS is an opinion; cash is a fact. Buffett's 1986 letter defines owner
earnings: reported earnings plus depreciation/amortization and other non-cash
charges, MINUS the capitalized spending required to fully maintain unit volume
and competitive position (maintenance capex), adjusted for needed working-capital
growth. This is the amount an owner could extract annually without impairing the
franchise. Disciplines that follow: (a) depreciation is usually a REAL cost —
businesses claiming "EBITDA" as earnings are asking you to ignore the tooth
fairy's bill; (b) stock compensation is a real expense however the income
statement dresses it; (c) distinguish maintenance from growth capex explicitly —
management rarely does; (d) rising receivables/inventory against flat revenue is
earnings borrowed from the future. Value the business as the discounted stream of
owner earnings, using a demanding rate; if the arithmetic needs a spreadsheet's
tenth column of compounding growth to work, the answer is no.

## Economic goodwill, moats, and pricing power

The 1983 letter's key distinction: accounting goodwill is an artifact; ECONOMIC
goodwill is the durable ability to earn returns on tangible capital far above
the cost of capital — created by franchises (needed/desired product, no close
substitute, unregulated pricing) and defended by moats: brand share-of-mind,
network effects, regulatory license, scale economies, switching costs, and
low-cost production. Tests the analyst should run: Has the company raised prices
ahead of inflation without volume loss (the one-question moat test)? Does high
ROIC persist over a decade (mean reversion is the base rate; persistence is the
exception demanding explanation)? Does the moat survive a well-funded competitor
deliberately attacking it? Inflation insight: franchises with pricing power and
low tangible-capital intensity are inflation-protected compounders, while
capital-heavy commodity businesses must reinvest ever-more dollars just to stand
still — inflation is a tax on their owners. Prefer businesses that grow value
with retained pennies, not retained fortunes.

## Return on invested capital and the reinvestment engine

The compounding machine has two gears: the rate earned on incremental invested
capital, and the fraction of earnings that can be reinvested at that rate. A
business earning 25% on incremental capital that can redeploy most of its
earnings for a decade is worth a large multiple of a business earning 8% however
cheap the latter looks. Compute ROIC honestly: NOPAT over tangible invested
capital (working capital + net fixed assets), excluding purchased goodwill when
judging the business, including it when judging management's acquisitions. Watch
the INCREMENTAL ratio: growth capex over added NOPAT across 3–5 year windows.
The gravest value destruction hides in high-ROIC companies reinvesting at
low-ROIC: the average masks the margin. When reinvestment runway is exhausted,
value depends on capital allocation — which is the next section.

## Capital allocation — judging management like an investor

CEOs are capital allocators first; most arrive untrained. The five choices:
reinvest, acquire, pay down debt, dividend, repurchase. The report card: every
retained dollar must create at least a dollar of market value over time (the
one-dollar test, applied over five-year rolling windows). Buybacks are brilliant
below conservative intrinsic value and destructive above it — test whether
management states a valuation discipline or repurchases on autopilot at any
price. Acquisitions: the base rate is value destruction; be suspicious of serial
acquirers, "synergy" arithmetic, and deals priced in the acquirer's undervalued
stock. Watch for the institutional imperative (1989 letter): the organizational
gravity by which companies imitate peers, expand for size not returns, and staff
studies exist to justify what the boss already wants. Candor is the proxy: does
management discuss mistakes plainly, account for stock comp as cost, and write to
owners as partners? A management that lies to shareholders about small things
will lie about large ones.

## Reading the filings — footnotes as the crime scene

The income statement is the company's story about itself; the footnotes are the
evidence. Non-negotiable reads: revenue recognition policy (aggressiveness =
future restatement risk); pension/OPEB assumptions (a 1% optimistic discount
rate manufactures earnings); operating-lease and purchase obligations (real
debt wherever the accountants file it); stock-compensation tables (dilution is
compensation; add it back to cost); litigation and contingencies; related-party
transactions (self-dealing lives here); segment data (where the moat actually
is, and what the conglomerate average conceals); auditor changes and internal-
control opinions. Cash-flow forensics: net income persistently above operating
cash flow, receivables/inventory growing faster than sales, serial "one-time"
charges, and capitalized costs that peers expense — each is earnings quality
decaying before the headline breaks. The 10-K is read back-to-front: proxy
(incentives), then footnotes, then MD&A, then the numbers, then — last — the
letter to shareholders.

## Circle of competence and the too-hard pile

The size of the circle matters far less than knowing its boundary. Operational
test: can you explain, in one paragraph without jargon, why the customers of
this business will still be paying it in ten years, what could kill it, and
roughly what it must earn to justify its price? If not, it goes in the too-hard
pile without shame — there are no called strikes in investing. The circle grows
by reading (filings, industry primers, trade press) and by post-mortems on your
own decisions, not by borrowing conviction from others. Beware competence
inflation during bull markets: the feeling of understanding correlates with
recent price action, not with knowledge.

## Temperament, patience, and the behavior of the crowd

Be fearful when others are greedy, greedy when others are fearful — as a
PROCESS, not a slogan: pre-commit appraisal and buy prices before the storm so
action under stress is mechanical; hold cash without discomfort when nothing
clears the hurdle (cash is a call option on future fear); treat forecasting of
markets, rates, and elections as noise (forecasts tell you about the forecaster).
The investor's chief problem — and even his worst enemy — is likely himself:
envy (someone else getting rich faster), leverage (the only way a smart person
goes broke), and activity for its own sake (the punch-card discipline: imagine a
lifetime limit of twenty decisions). Time horizon is the last honest edge: value
a business over decades in a market that prices it over quarters.

## Graham's defensive and enterprising frameworks

Graham split investors by effort, not intelligence. The DEFENSIVE investor's
protection is rules: adequate size, strong balance sheet (current ratio > 2 for
industrials), a decade of unbroken earnings and dividends, moderate P/E on
average (not peak) earnings, and dollar-cost averaging into a fixed stock/bond
split (25–75% band, rebalanced by policy not prediction). The ENTERPRISING
investor earns extra return only through extra work in unpopular places:
out-of-favor large caps, secondary issues below appraised value, workouts and
special situations, and — the deepest safety Graham found — net-nets: price
below net current asset value (current assets minus ALL liabilities), a diversified
basket of businesses priced below liquidation. Modern translation: classic
net-nets are rare in large caps; the ENTERPRISE of the enterprising investor is
now footnote-level earnings-quality work, spinoffs, and forced-seller situations.
Never occupy the middle: half-effort with full-risk exposure is the worst seat.

## Valuation arithmetic without false precision

Intrinsic value is the discounted value of cash that can be taken out of a
business during its remaining life — an ESTIMATE, honest only as a range.
Practical hierarchy: (1) normalize owner earnings across a cycle; (2) separate
the value of current earning power (owner earnings / demanding rate) from the
value of PROFITABLE growth (growth is only worth paying for when reinvestment
returns exceed the hurdle — growth at cost-of-capital is worth zero, below it
negative); (3) sanity-check against private-owner value (what an informed buyer
of the whole company would pay) and asset replacement value; (4) require today's
price to sit well below the LOW end of the range. Reject false precision: two
analysts with the same spreadsheet and different terminal assumptions differ by
2x; therefore prefer situations where the decision survives crude arithmetic.
If it needs a DCF to look cheap, it isn't.

## Selling discipline and the mistakes catalog

Sell when: the thesis breaks (moat breached, capital allocation turns hostile,
earnings quality rots), the price grossly exceeds the high end of appraised
value, or a decisively better opportunity requires the capital. Do NOT sell
merely because price rose, or fell — re-run the appraisal instead. The mistakes
worth cataloging from the letters: buying fair businesses at wonderful prices
(the cigar-butt trap — time is the enemy of the poor business and the friend of
the wonderful one, so the mediocre bargain decays while you wait); confusing a
cyclical peak for a plateau; thumb-sucking (recognized opportunities not acted
on); selling the wonderful compounder for a taxable 30% gain; and airline-shaped
industries where a century of growth never produced owner profit — growth is not
a synonym for value. Keep a written decision journal: entry thesis, kill
criteria, appraisal range. The journal, reread annually, is the only honest
teacher.

## Float, insurance thinking, and structural advantage

Buffett's meta-lesson: seek structures where money works for you before you owe
it. Insurance float — premiums held before claims — is borrowed money at
negative cost when underwriting is disciplined; the general principle is
negative working-capital businesses (customers pay first), and any structure
converting other people's money into investable capital without covenant risk.
Corollary discipline from underwriting: price for the risk or write no business;
volume targets corrupt underwriting exactly as growth targets corrupt capital
allocation. The analyst's use: identify who in an industry holds the float-like
position (deposits, deferred revenue, marketplace escrow) — that player compounds
on a tailwind competitors lack.
